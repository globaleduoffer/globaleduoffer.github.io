Complete Guide to China Apostille

Understand China apostille requirements, eligible documents and the correct route for using Chinese public documents overseas.

What Is a China Apostille?

An apostille certifies the authenticity of the signature, seal or stamp on a public document for use in another country that participates in the Hague Apostille Convention. It does not certify that every statement in the underlying document is true.

A China apostille may be needed when a China-issued public document is submitted overseas for immigration, study, employment, family, professional or commercial purposes. After the Convention entered into force for China, an apostille replaced the traditional multi-step consular legalization chain for eligible documents used in participating destination countries.

The destination country and receiving authority should always be checked first. Not every country accepts apostilles, not every Chinese document can be apostilled in its original form, and an apostille does not automatically replace translation, notarization or other supporting materials.

If your document was issued outside China and will be used in mainland China, see our guide to foreign documents for use in mainland China.

When Is an Apostille Required?

An apostille is generally used when a public document issued or properly prepared in China must be presented in another Convention country and the receiving organization asks for authenticated evidence of the signature, seal or official capacity attached to it.

Check before applying. The correct route depends on the destination, document type, issuing authority and intended use. A university, immigration authority, court, employer or company registry may set its own document-format, translation and issue-date requirements.

Applicants living overseas may sometimes arrange an apostille through an authorized channel or representative, but availability and supporting documents vary by authority and document. Remote submission should never be assumed without confirming the applicable procedure.

Which Chinese Documents Can Be Apostilled?

Many public or notarized Chinese documents can often be prepared for apostille, depending on their form, issuing body and destination. Some originals may first need notarization, a certified copy or another preliminary step.

Personal and civil documents

  • Birth certificates and marriage certificates — see the China marriage certificate Apostille guide
  • Divorce certificates or court documents
  • Police or no criminal record certificates
  • Medical certificates
  • Household or identity-related records, where applicable

Education documents

  • Degree and graduation certificates
  • Academic transcripts
  • School or university records
  • Professional qualification certificates

For academic-document context, see our Degree Certification Guide.

Commercial and corporate documents

  • Business licenses and certificates of incorporation
  • Powers of attorney and board resolutions
  • Commercial agreements
  • Company registration documents
  • Other notarized corporate documents

Requirements can differ by province or city, document issuer and country of use. A document that is acceptable for one purpose may need a different certified or notarized version for another.

How the China Apostille Process Works

The China apostille process should begin with the receiving authority's written requirements, not with assumptions about the document. A typical route may include the following steps:

  1. Confirm whether the destination country accepts apostilles.
  2. Confirm the receiving authority's exact requirements.
  3. Identify whether the document is public, notarized or needs prior notarization.
  4. Prepare the original or an acceptable certified copy.
  1. Arrange translation if the receiving authority requires it.
  2. Submit through the appropriate Chinese authority or authorized channel.
  3. Check names, dates, signatures, seals and apostille details after issuance.
  4. Submit the completed document to the receiving authority.

This sequence is a general guide. The required original, certified copy, notarization, translation and submission channel may vary, and not every authority offers online or fully remote processing.

Translation, Notarization and Supporting Documents

An original document is the record issued by the relevant body. A certified copy is a formally confirmed copy, while a notarized document records a notarial act or certification. A translation renders the text in another language; a notarized translation adds a separate notarial step concerning the translation or translator, as applicable.

What an apostille does

An apostille generally authenticates the signature, seal or official capacity attached to a public document. Some Chinese records must first be notarized or converted into an acceptable notarial or certified form before an apostille can be issued.

What an apostille does not do

It does not automatically authenticate the truth of the document's content, include a translation or make every copy acceptable. A receiving authority may request a translation, translator statement, notarized translation or other supporting material. Review what to check if an Apostilled Chinese document is not accepted overseas before assuming the Apostille itself is invalid.

The correct sequence depends on the document and destination. Translation may occur before or after other steps, so applicants should not assume that notarization equals apostille or that one standard order applies everywhere.

Processing Time and Common Delays

China apostille processing time varies by document type, region and competent authority. Prior notarization, certified-copy preparation or translation may add time, and commercial documents may follow a different route from personal documents.

Typical timelines are estimates only. Missing information, unclear or damaged seals, holiday periods, archive checks and additional document requests may delay completion. Applicants should also allow time to correct the following common issues:

  • Using apostille when embassy legalization is required
  • Applying before checking the destination requirements
  • Submitting an original when a notarized copy is requested
  • Missing notarization or an incomplete translation
  • Mismatched names, passport numbers or dates
  • Damaged documents or unclear signatures and seals
  • Assuming the apostille certifies document content
  • Ignoring document validity or issue-date rules
  • Using a record that covers the wrong period or purpose
  • Leaving insufficient time for corrections

Apostille vs Embassy Legalization

Apostille and embassy legalization are different procedures. One should not be used when the other is required; the destination country and receiving organization determine the correct route, and some documents or uses may involve additional requirements.

Apostille

Generally used between countries participating in the Hague Apostille Convention. In applicable cases, it replaces the traditional multi-step consular legalization chain.

It does not automatically remove notarization, translation or document-format requirements.

Embassy or consular legalization

May still be required when the destination does not accept apostilles or the receiving authority specifies a consular route.

The process may involve notarization, authentication and embassy or consular legalization, depending on the destination and document.

For a closer comparison, read the Embassy Legalization Guide. If you need help coordinating the apostille route, see our China Hague Apostille Services.

Need to confirm apostille vs legalization?

Review the destination and receiving authority before choosing a route. If the written instructions are unclear, obtain confirmation before preparing the document.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a China apostille?

A China apostille verifies the authenticity of a signature, seal, stamp or official capacity attached to a Chinese public document. It is generally used when the document will be submitted in another country that accepts apostilles under the Hague Apostille Convention. It does not certify that the information contained in the underlying document is true.

Which Chinese documents can be apostilled?

Personal, educational and commercial documents may be eligible, depending on their form and issuing authority. Examples can include notarized birth, marriage and police certificates, degree or transcript documents, powers of attorney and company records. Some originals require prior notarization, a certified copy or other preparation, so eligibility should be checked for the specific document and intended destination.

Do Chinese documents need notarization before apostille?

Not always. Some documents may follow a direct public-document route, while others must first be notarized or prepared as a notarized or certified copy. The correct sequence depends on the document type, issuing authority, available original and receiving country's requirements. Applicants should confirm the accepted format before arranging either notarization or apostille.

Does a China apostille include translation?

No. An apostille does not automatically include a translation. The receiving organization may still require the Chinese document and apostille to be translated, and some cases may require a translator's statement or notarized translation. Translation timing and format vary, so the receiving authority's instructions should be checked before the documents are prepared.

How long does the China apostille process take?

Processing time varies by document type, location and responsible authority. Prior notarization, certified-copy preparation, translation, missing information, unclear seals, holidays or additional document requests may extend the timeline. Because different documents can follow different routes, applicants should treat any stated timeframe as an estimate and allow time for corrections.

Is apostille the same as embassy legalization?

No. Apostille and embassy legalization are different procedures. Apostille is generally used for eligible documents moving between countries that accept apostilles, while embassy or consular legalization may be required for other destinations. The receiving country and receiving organization determine the correct route, and either process may still involve notarization, translation or supporting documents.

Can a China apostille be used in every country?

No. Apostilles are generally intended for use in countries that participate in and apply the Hague Apostille Convention. Acceptance also depends on the document and the receiving organization. Before applying, confirm the current requirement with the relevant authority, embassy, consulate or recipient rather than assuming that an apostille will be accepted everywhere.

Can I obtain an apostille after leaving China?

In some cases, yes. An applicant living overseas may be able to use an authorized representative or suitable document-assistance process in China. Availability depends on the document type, whether the original is accessible, prior notarization needs and local authority requirements. Not every authority offers remote processing, so the route and authorization documents should be confirmed first.

Important: Apostille, notarization, translation and legalization requirements vary by document type, destination country and receiving organization. Applicants should confirm the latest requirements with the relevant authority, embassy, consulate, legal representative or receiving organization before submission.
